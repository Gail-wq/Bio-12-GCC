    Exam strategies

    The three Level 2 Biology externals are sat together in one end-of-year session. Each paper has three questions, and each question is marked out of 8 (N0 to E8). These strategies are built around how markers actually award grades.

    Read the command word, then pitch your answer

    GradeCommand wordsWhat the marker wantsSentence starter
    AIdentify, state, define, describe, label, calculateCorrect terms, facts and characteristics. What happens.“Transcription is… It occurs in the…”
    MExplain, give reasonsHow or why it happens, using a biological mechanism and the context in the question.“This happens because… which means…”
    EDiscuss, compare, contrast, justify, evaluate, analyseTwo or more ideas linked together, with consequences for the organism or population, supported by the data.“Therefore… In contrast… As a result, the population/organism will…”

    The “because chain”

    Most Merit and Excellence marks are lost because the chain stops too early. Push every point through four links:

    What→How→Why→So what?

    Example: “A substitution changes one base (what), so one codon on the mRNA changes (how). A different tRNA binds, so a different amino acid is added (why). The protein folds differently, its active site changes shape and the enzyme cannot bind its substrate, so the pathway stops (so what).”

    Use the bullet points as your plan

    • Number the bullet points in the question and tick each one off as you answer it.
    • The last bullet is almost always the Excellence one. Plan it before you write.
    • Name the organism, gene or data from the stimulus in every paragraph. Generic answers cap at Merit.
    • Annotated diagrams count as evidence. Label and explain, don’t just draw.

    Timing in the session

    • Spend roughly equal time on each paper you are sitting, then keep 10 minutes to check.
    • About 15 to 18 minutes per question. If you are stuck, write what you know and move on.
    • Read all three questions first. Start with your strongest to build momentum.
    • Use the spare pages for a two-minute plan on discuss questions.

    Graph and data questions

    • Describe the trend, quote two data points with units, then explain the biology.
    • For plateaus, name the limiting factor and say why it now limits the rate.
    • For calculations, show working and units. Round sensibly.
    • For Punnett squares, show gametes, the grid and the ratio as a phenotype ratio.

    Common traps from our 2026 papers and NZQA reports

    • Calling the stroma “cytoplasm”. The stroma is the fluid inside the chloroplast.
    • Explaining the light reactions when the question asks how the structure of the thylakoid or stroma helps photosynthesis.
    • Not linking light-dependent and light-independent stages to the parts of a rate graph, and not naming the limiting factor.
    • Active transport answers that miss the carrier protein pump, or don’t explain that ATP changes the pump’s shape.
    • Forgetting that osmosis is passive and uses no ATP.
    • Saying mitosis “makes new cells”. It produces two genetically identical daughter cells for growth and repair.
    • Saying an enzyme “dies”. Explain that high temperature or the wrong pH breaks bonds, changing the active site so the substrate no longer fits.
    • Writing about things the question did not ask (for example inhibitors or cofactors when they weren’t in the question).
    • Mixing up gene, allele and chromosome, or homozygous and homologous.
    • Saying a mutation “is always harmful”. Many are neutral; some are beneficial in a given environment.

      How we learn in Year 12 Biology

      Study toolkit

      The routines behind this site, and how to use them at home. Each one is based on strong evidence about how memory and understanding build.

      Retrieval practice

      Pulling knowledge out of memory strengthens it far more than re-reading. Start every study session with the Do Now on the home page or a flashcard round, before you open your notes.

      Spaced practice

      Revisit each topic after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week and 3 weeks. Older topics (osmosis, cellular processes) need a short weekly review even while we are learning genetics.

      I do, we do, you do

      Worksheets move from a worked example, to a partly completed one, to a blank task. If the blank task is hard, step back to the worked example rather than guessing.

      Dual coding

      Pair words with diagrams. For every process, draw it from memory with labels, then write the explanation next to each label. Annotated diagrams also earn marks in the exam.

      Blurting

      Set a 5-minute timer and write everything you know about one success criterion. Check against the notes in a different colour. The gaps are your study list.

      Self-assessment

      Use the Self-check tab on each topic to traffic-light the success criteria. Your ratings save on this device so you can track progress across the year.

      From Achieved to Excellence: SOLO and NCEA

      SOLO levelNCEA gradeWhat it looks like in Biology

      RelationalMExplains how and why one process causes another, using the context given.
      Extended abstractELinks several processes, compares, predicts consequences, evaluates or justifies with data.

      Sentence starters for Merit and Excellence

      • “This occurs because…”
      • “As a result of this,…”
      • “This is important for the cell / organism because…”
      • “In comparison,… whereas…”
      • “Both… however only…”
      • “Over many generations, the frequency of the allele will…”
      • “The data supports this because at… the value was…”

        For teachers: how the resources are designed

        • Learning intentions and success criteria for each topic are written at A, M and E level so students can see the progression.
        • Exemplar answers show the same question at all three grades, with a note on what lifts each answer to the next grade. Students attempt first, then compare.
        • Worksheets give immediate feedback (check buttons) and can be printed as clean handouts.
        • Flashcards and the Do Now draw on one shared glossary, supporting interleaved retrieval across topics.
        • Case studies (the Fugates of Troublesome Creek and the Ulas family) connect genotype, environment and population genetics to real people. Approach them with respect: they are families, not curiosities.
